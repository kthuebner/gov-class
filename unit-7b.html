Read It Critically

Clear, memorable charts are also arguments. Before you accept this one, think about three things.

It started as a British chart

The designer is British, and the original party row listed Labour and Communism. This version swaps in American groups, but American parties are broad coalitions, and each includes people who disagree with parts of this chart.

Generalization or stereotype?

A generalization describes a pattern, is backed by data, and allows exceptions: "Most Republicans favor the death penalty" (84%, Pew). A stereotype applies a fixed image to everyone: "Liberals oppose the death penalty," when about half of Democrats support it. Ask: Is it based on data? Does it allow exceptions? Would you assume it about someone you just met?

Averages hide the middle

Each party has real internal splits, and many people hold views from both columns. Libertarians, populists, and independents often land on both sides. Part 3 shows one way to see them.

Questions for discussion

  1. Pick a row and rewrite each side in words its own supporters would use. What changed?
  2. Find one row that reads as a fair generalization and one that reads as a stereotype. What makes the difference?
  3. Did you land in both columns? What does that say about describing people with one line?
