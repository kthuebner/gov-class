Read It Critically

This graphic is popular because it is clear and memorable. That clarity comes at a cost. Every choice about which words to use and which traits to include is an argument. Before you accept it as a description of real people, look at how it was built.

It started as a British chart

Labour is the British left party, and the ends of the party list (Communism on the left, Nationalist on the right) are not the mirror images of the Democratic and Republican parties. American parties are broad coalitions, and each one holds people who disagree with parts of this chart.

The family section is one theory

The nurturing parent and strict parent models come from linguist George Lakoff's book Moral Politics (1996). It is an influential theory, but a contested one, and Lakoff wrote openly as a progressive. Jonathan Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory is a leading alternative that explains the same divide in different terms. The graphic presents the theory as fact.

Who would describe themselves this way?

Many words on the right side are ones conservatives would reject: exclusive, aggression, militancy, respect and fear, survival of the fittest, no work ethic. The left side mostly gets words its own supporters would choose: fulfilled, creates potential, inclusive. There are jabs in the other direction too. "Tax and spend" is a phrase critics of the left use, and "interfere" is not a neutral verb. A fair comparison lets each side describe itself.

Some labels do not fit

"Equity" sits on the right, but in current American usage the word is strongly associated with the left. A "scientific" religion is hard to define. The urban and rural job lists are stereotypes. Plenty of teachers are conservative, and plenty of police officers are liberal.

The original numbers had no source trail

The original graphic gave percentages with no year, no question wording, and no definition of "left" or "right." They have been replaced here with Pew Research Center figures that you can check. Some of the originals were far off. The graphic put the left's support for the death penalty at 30%. Pew's 2026 figure for Democrats is 51%.

Averages hide the middle

Pew measures party identification, not ideology. Within each party there are real splits. Pew found that moderate and liberal Republicans are about evenly divided on abortion, and Democrats are almost evenly split on the death penalty. A two-sided chart also leaves out libertarians, populists, and independents, who often mix positions from both columns.

Questions for discussion

  1. Pick three rows from the chart. Rewrite each side in words its own supporters would use. What changed, and why does it matter?
  2. Compare the original percentages with the Pew figures. Which ones were furthest off? What does that suggest about how the designers saw each side?
  3. Where do your own views land? Did you find yourself in both columns? What does that say about using a single left to right line to describe people?
