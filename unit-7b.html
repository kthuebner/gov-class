Read It Critically

This graphic is popular because it is clear and memorable. That clarity comes at a cost. Every choice about which words to use and which traits to include is an argument. Before you accept it as a description of real people, look at how it was built.

It started as a British chart

The graphic comes from a British designer. Its party row originally listed Labour, the British left party, and ran from Communism on the left to Nationalist on the right. This version swaps in American groups, but a single row still cannot show how far apart these groups really are. American parties are broad coalitions, and each one holds people who disagree with parts of this chart.

The family section is one theory

The nurturing parent and strict parent models come from linguist George Lakoff's book Moral Politics (1996). It is an influential theory, but a contested one, and Lakoff wrote openly as a progressive. Jonathan Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory is a leading alternative that explains the same divide in different terms. The graphic presents the theory as fact.

Who would describe themselves this way?

Many words on the right side are ones conservatives would reject: exclusive, aggression, militancy, respect and fear, survival of the fittest. The left side mostly gets words its own supporters would choose: fulfilled, creates potential, inclusive. There are jabs in the other direction too. "Tax and spend" is a phrase critics of the left use, and "interfere" is not a neutral verb. A fair comparison lets each side describe itself.

Some labels do not fit

"Equity" sits on the right, but in current American usage the word is strongly associated with the left. The original Religion row called the left's religion "scientific," which is hard to define, so that row has been rewritten here. The urban and rural job lists are stereotypes. Plenty of teachers are conservative, and plenty of police officers are liberal.

Generalization or stereotype?

A generalization describes a pattern in a group, is backed by evidence, and allows for exceptions: "Most Republicans favor the death penalty" (Pew puts it at 84%). A stereotype is a fixed, oversimplified image applied to everyone in a group, often with no evidence: "Liberals oppose the death penalty," when about half of Democrats actually support it. Three questions help tell them apart. Is it based on data? Does it allow for exceptions? Would you assume it about a person you just met? This graphic often slides from the first into the second by presenting a tendency as if it defined every member of a side.

Averages hide the middle

Pew measures party identification, not ideology. Within each party there are real splits. Pew found that moderate and liberal Republicans are about evenly divided on abortion, and Democrats are almost evenly split on the death penalty. A two-sided chart also leaves out libertarians, populists, and independents, who often mix positions from both columns.

Questions for discussion

  1. Pick three rows from the chart. Rewrite each side in words its own supporters would use. What changed, and why does it matter?
  2. Find one row that reads as a fair generalization and one that reads as a stereotype. What makes the difference?
  3. Where do your own views land? Did you find yourself in both columns? What does that say about using a single left to right line to describe people?
